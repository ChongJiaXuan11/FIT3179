Through the Lens

What Australia's Wildlife Photos Reveal

Every year, millions of Australians point a camera at a bird, a lizard, a wildflower, or a passing whale, and upload it. What started as a hobby has quietly become one of the richest sources of wildlife data scientists have ever had access to: every geotagged photo is a timestamped record of a species existing at a place, whether the person taking it realised it or not.

But not everything gets photographed equally. Some animals dominate the frame while others, including species on the brink of extinction, are barely documented at all. This visualisation traces which animals and plants get the spotlight, where the cameras cluster, and what the gaps in the record mean for the species conservation depends on us noticing.

Section 1: What Gets Photographed

Plants carry the highest photo count of any group on this page, outpacing birds, mammals, reptiles, and amphibians even when those four are added together. Most people assume birds or mammals dominate wildlife photography, since they're usually the animals that catch our eye first. This chart shows that assumption simply doesn't hold up.

Each line connects a species' documentation rank on the left to its threat severity rank on the right, so a steep, crossing line signals a real mismatch between the two. The Eastern Curlew makes the point clearly, it's Critically Endangered, yet sits near the bottom for photo coverage, making it one of the least-documented species shown here.

Each small square stands for one species, colored by how well-documented it is, blue for plenty of photos, pale blue for just a few, and red for none at all.

Birds and Amphibians carry the most red squares by far, with roughly 38% of species in each group never having been photographed, even once.

Drag the slider to move between years and watch how many photos were submitted each month for every taxon group, revealing the seasonal rhythm hidden inside the data.

Plants reliably spike every springduring wildflower season, while insect photography drops off sharply each winter, a pattern that repeats almost identically from one year to the next.

Section 2: Where Photography Happens

Each circle sits over a state, sized by its total photo record count, so the larger the circle, the more photos were submitted from that part of the country.

New South Wales clearly holds the largest circle here, meaning the highest raw total, though this view only counts totals and says nothing about population size.

Each shape on this map marks one of Australia's 89 official ecological regions, shaded by how many photo records have come from inside its borders, with darker blue meaning more.

South Eastern Queensland stands out as the single most-photographed region on the entire map, holding well over a million records, more than double its closest competitor.

This map divides each state's photo count by its population, so the shading reflects photos per person rather than a simple raw total across the whole state.

Once population enters the picture, the Northern Territory and Tasmania turn the darkest red on the map, revealing individual engagement far higher than in the bigger eastern states.

Each dot stands for roughly 75 bird photo records, so wherever dots cluster densely together, that area simply saw more photos taken during that particular season.

Notice how dots spread along the northern coastline specifically in winter, as tourists travel north chasing warm, dry-season weather and bring their cameras along for the trip.

This chart ranks the 15 national parks with the highest number of photo records per 1,000 hectares, letting small parks with heavy foot traffic stand out against much larger, quieter reserves.

Dandenong Ranges, just outside Melbourne, comes out clearly on top, not because it holds anything ecologically rare, but simply because it's easy for city residents to reach on a weekend.

Section 3: The Conservation Argument

Each bar represents one state or territory split into two parts, purple marks species that state alone considers threatened, and brown marks species also protected under federal law.

Queensland carries the largest red segment of any state, with 2,290 species it treats as threatened receiving no federal protection whatsoever, more than anywhere else in the country.

Each small square represents one combination of taxon group and threat level, with darker red meaning more species fall into that particular combination across the dataset.

Plants dominate nearly every panel here, simply because plants make up most of Australia's official threatened species list, not because they happen to be well photographed.

Follow the flowing bands from left to right to trace how species move from their taxon group, through their official threat status, into how well they've actually been photographed.

Being Critically Endangered doesn't appear to guarantee strong photo coverage at all, since the flow shows no consistent link between how threatened a species is and how documented it becomes.

Each small panel tracks one taxon group's cumulative photo count from 2010 through 2025, and because the values stack up over time, every line can only climb, never fall.

Plants show by far the steepest climb, logging roughly 32 times more photos in 2025 than in 2010, while amphibians and mammals barely moved across the same fifteen years.

Conclusion

What emerges from this data isn't a simple story of neglect — it's a story of uneven attention. Plants and insects, not the birds and mammals most people associate with wildlife photography, generate the largest share of Australia's citizen-science record. Photography clusters where people already are: the parks that top our density rankings sit on the fringes of Melbourne, Sydney and Adelaide, not inside the continent's most biodiverse or ecologically significant wilderness.

Counterintuitively, the Northern Territory and Tasmania — not the populous eastern states — show the highest photographic density per person. The conservation picture is more sobering: roughly one in twenty of Australia's threatened species has never been photographed, and the gap is sharpest among Critically Endangered birds. Citizen-science volume has grown more than thirtyfold since 2010 — proof the opportunity isn't to photograph more, but to photograph differently.
